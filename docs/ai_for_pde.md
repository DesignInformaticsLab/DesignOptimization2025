## Part 1: AI is data and algorithm compression

### Data and algorithms have structure

A central thesis of modern AI: **data and algorithms generated by nature have
low-rank structure**, and AI discovers and exploits that structure. In other
words, AI is *compression*.

**Unsupervised learning finds intrinsic dimensions.** PCA discovers the linear
subspace where data lives; t-SNE finds low-dimensional embeddings of
high-dimensional data like handwritten digits (0–9).

> NP-hardness describes *worst-case* complexity. Real-world instances are
> structured. A learning algorithm trained on the natural distribution can
> discover the input-output mapping — without simulating the mechanism that
> generates it.

## Part 2: Partial differential equations and the curse of dimensionality

### What is a PDE?

A second-order linear PDE has three types of terms, each with a distinct
physical effect:

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \underbrace{\nu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}}_{\text{diffusion}} + \underbrace{\beta \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}}_{\text{convection}} + \underbrace{\rho \, u}_{\text{reaction}}
$$

| Term | Order | Physical effect | Visualization |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Diffusion** ($u_{xx}$) | 2nd | Smoothing — peaks flatten, sharp features blur | A dye drop spreading in still water |
| **Convection** ($u_x$) | 1st | Transport — the profile slides rigidly in one direction | A leaf drifting on a river current |
| **Reaction** ($u$) | 0th | Local growth/decay — amplitude scales up or down in place | Bacteria multiplying independently |

**Diffusion** looks at the *curvature* of the solution. If a point is higher
than its neighbors, diffusion pulls it down; if lower, it pulls it up. Over
time, everything tends toward a uniform state.

**Convection** looks at the *slope*. It pushes the entire profile sideways at
speed $\beta$. The shape doesn't change — it just moves.

**Reaction** looks only at the *value* at each point. Each point grows or decays
independently of its neighbors.

### The curse of dimensionality

Traditional PDE solvers discretize the domain on a grid. The number of grid
points scales as $N^d$ where $d$ is the spatial dimension — **exponential** in
dimension. This is the *curse of dimensionality* (CoD).

```{figure} _static/pinn_figs/cod_grid.png
:name: fig-cod-grid
:width: 70%
:align: center

Grid points grow exponentially with dimension: $N$ in 1D, $N^2$ in 2D, $N^3$
in 3D.
```

The CoD is the bottleneck for many important application domains:

- **Autonomous driving and robotics** — Hamilton-Jacobi reachability analysis
  for multi-vehicle collision avoidance (DeepReach: 9D multi-vehicle, 10D
  narrow passage problems; Bansal & Tomlin, 2021)
- **Computational finance** — Black-Scholes for multi-asset option pricing
  (dimension = number of assets)
- **Quantum mechanics** — many-body Schrödinger equation (dimension = number
  of particles)

```{figure} _static/pinn_figs/cod_applications.jpg
:name: fig-cod-apps
:width: 60%
:align: center

Applications limited by the curse of dimensionality.
```

**How PINNs help with CoD:**

| PDE class | Method | Scaling result | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kolmogorov (heat, Black-Scholes, HJB) | PINN (residual) | Error scales **polynomially** in $d$ | Mishra & Molinaro, 2022 |
| Elliptic (Poisson, static Schrödinger) | Deep Ritz (variational) | Error is **dimension-independent** under Barron-space regularity | Lu, Lu & Wang, 2021 |
| Hyperbolic (wave, deterministic HJ), Navier-Stokes | PINN | Empirical success, **no proven dimension scaling** yet | — |

PINN advantages over mesh-based solvers:
1. **Mesh-free formulation** — no grid, no exponential grid-point explosion
2. **Adaptive sampling** — collocation points concentrate where the residual is
   high, not uniformly over the domain
3. **Low-rank exploitation** — neural networks implicitly exploit the low-rank
   structure of PDE solutions

## Part 3: Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)

### How PINNs work

A PINN approximates the PDE solution $u(x,t)$ with a neural network
$u_\theta(x,t)$ and trains it by minimizing a composite loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{bc}}(\theta)}_{\text{boundary/initial conditions}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{pde}}(\theta)}_{\text{PDE residual at interior points}}
$$

where:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pde}} = \frac{1}{N_r} \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} \left| \mathcal{N}[u_\theta](x_i, t_i) \right|^2
$$

Here $\mathcal{N}[u_\theta]$ is the PDE residual — the amount by which the
network's output violates the differential equation at collocation point
$(x_i, t_i)$. The derivatives $\partial u_\theta / \partial x$,
$\partial^2 u_\theta / \partial x^2$, etc., are computed via **automatic
differentiation** through the network.

**Key idea:** No mesh is needed. Collocation points are scattered freely in the
domain, and the PDE is enforced as a soft constraint in the loss function.

### Neural operators (PINO)

While a PINN solves *one* PDE instance (fixed coefficients and boundary
conditions), a **neural operator** learns the solution *operator* — a mapping
from input functions (e.g., initial/boundary conditions, forcing terms, PDE
coefficients) to the solution function. Once trained, it generalizes to new
problem instances without retraining.

The **Fourier Neural Operator** (FNO; Li et al., 2021) is the most widely used
architecture. It operates in the frequency domain:

$$
v_{l+1}(x) = \sigma \left( W_l \, v_l(x) + \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[ R_l \cdot \mathcal{F}[v_l] \right](x) \right)
$$

where $\mathcal{F}$ is the Fourier transform, $R_l$ is a learnable filter in
frequency space, and $W_l$ is a local linear transform. The key advantage:
global information propagation at each layer via the Fourier modes, at
$O(N \log N)$ cost.

**Physics-Informed Neural Operator (PINO)** combines the FNO architecture with
PINN-style physics losses, enabling training with little or no labeled data
(Li et al., 2024).

### Galerkin PINN (variational form)

Instead of enforcing the PDE pointwise (strong form), Galerkin PINNs enforce it
in *weak form*: multiply by test functions $v_k$ and integrate:

$$
\int_\Omega \mathcal{N}[u_\theta] \, v_k \, dx = 0, \quad k = 1, \ldots, K
$$

Integration by parts reduces the derivative order the network must represent
(e.g., from 2nd to 1st order), improving stability. The integrals are computed
via Gauss quadrature on subdomains.

The state-of-the-art is **hp-VPINN** (Kharazmi et al., 2021):
- **h-refinement:** decompose the domain into subdomains
- **p-refinement:** use Legendre polynomial test functions of increasing degree
- Integrate with Gauss-Lobatto-Jacobi quadrature

A key finding (Berrone et al., 2022): for smooth solutions, the best strategy is
**low-degree test functions with high-precision quadrature** — the quadrature
accuracy matters more than the test function degree.

### PINN learning dynamics via NTK analysis

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) provides a theoretical lens for understanding
PINN training dynamics (Wang et al., 2022a). In the infinite-width limit, the
training dynamics are governed by the NTK matrix:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}
\begin{bmatrix} \mathcal{L}_{\text{bc}} \\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{pde}} \end{bmatrix}
= -\begin{bmatrix} K_{uu} & K_{ur} \\ K_{ru} & K_{rr} \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \mathcal{L}_{\text{bc}} \\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{pde}} \end{bmatrix}
$$

where $K_{uu}$ is the NTK block for boundary points and $K_{rr}$ is the NTK
block for PDE residual points. The **eigenvalues** of these blocks control the
convergence rate of each loss component.

## Part 4: PINN challenges and solutions

### Challenge 1: Loss component imbalance

The eigenvalue spectra of $K_{uu}$ (boundary), $K_{u_t}$ (Neumann), and $K_r$
(PDE residual) have very different magnitudes. In particular, the PDE residual
eigenvalues are typically much larger because differential operators amplify the
NTK spectrum.

```{figure} _static/pinn_figs/ntk_eigenvalues.png
:name: fig-ntk-eigen
:width: 90%
:align: center

NTK eigenvalue spectra for Dirichlet BC ($K_u$), Neumann BC ($K_{u_t}$), and
PDE residual ($K_r$). The PDE residual eigenvalues are orders of magnitude
larger, causing it to dominate gradient updates while boundary conditions are
starved. Figure from Wang et al. (2022a).
```

**Consequence:** The PDE residual converges first while boundary conditions lag
behind — leading to solutions that approximately satisfy the PDE interior but
violate the boundary conditions, producing globally wrong solutions.

Each NTK block is also *individually ill-conditioned* (eigenvalue spread of
$10^8$–$10^{10}$). The root cause: smooth activations (tanh, sigmoid) produce
smooth NTK kernels, and smooth kernels inherently have rapidly decaying
eigenvalue spectra. This creates **spectral bias** — low-frequency components
are learned first, high-frequency components last or never.

**Solution: NTK-based adaptive weighting** (Wang et al., 2022a). Dynamically
reweight the loss components using the NTK eigenvalues:

$$
\mathcal{L} = w_{\text{bc}}(t) \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{bc}} + w_{\text{pde}}(t) \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{pde}}
$$

where $w_{\text{bc}} / w_{\text{pde}} \propto \lambda_{\max}(K_{rr}) /
\lambda_{\max}(K_{uu})$. This balances the learning speeds and achieves
**orders-of-magnitude improvement** in accuracy.

```{figure} _static/pinn_figs/ntk_adaptive_fix.png
:name: fig-ntk-fix
:width: 70%
:align: center

(Top) Fast residual convergence + slow boundary convergence = wrong solution.
(Bottom) NTK-based adaptive gradient scaling addresses this.
Figure from Wang et al. (2022a).
```

### Challenge 2: Failure on convection- and reaction-dominated PDEs

Even on simple 1D PDEs, PINNs fail when convection speed $\beta$ or reaction
rate $\rho$ is large (Krishnapriyan et al., 2021). The root cause is not lack of
network expressivity — the network *can* represent the correct solution — but
the **loss landscape becomes pathologically non-convex**, trapping the optimizer
in bad local minima.

```{figure} _static/pinn_figs/convection_failure.png
:name: fig-convection-fail
:width: 70%
:align: center

PINN failure on 1D convection with large convection coefficient ($\beta = 30$).
Left: exact solution. Right: PINN learns a nearly flat, wrong solution.
Figure from Krishnapriyan et al. (2021).
```

```{figure} _static/pinn_figs/reaction_diffusion_failure.png
:name: fig-reaction-fail
:width: 70%
:align: center

PINN failure on 1D reaction-diffusion with large coefficients ($\rho = 5$,
$\nu = 5$). Figure from Krishnapriyan et al. (2021).
```

**Solutions:**
1. **Curriculum regularization** — start with an easier PDE (low $\beta$ or
   $\rho$), then gradually increase to the target value
2. **Sequence-to-sequence learning** — solve in short time windows instead of
   predicting the entire space-time at once

Both achieve 1–2 orders of magnitude lower error than vanilla PINN training.

### Challenge 3: Causality violation in time-dependent PDEs

For time-dependent PDEs, standard PINNs try to learn the entire space-time
solution simultaneously. But physics is *causal* — the state at time $t$ depends
only on earlier times. Ignoring this allows the network to satisfy the PDE
residual at late times without correctly propagating information from the initial
condition (Wang et al., 2024).

```{figure} _static/pinn_figs/allen_cahn_failure.png
:name: fig-causality-fail
:width: 80%
:align: center

PINN failure on 1D Allen-Cahn equation: the solution at late times is wrong
because the network does not respect temporal causality.
Figure from Wang et al. (2024).
```

**Solution: Causal training** (Wang et al., 2024). Use time-dependent weights
that force the network to learn from initial/terminal boundaries first, then
propagate forward:

### Challenge 4: Solution gradient accuracy

Even when a PINN achieves low training loss on $u$, the predicted
*gradients* $\nabla_x u$ can be inaccurate. This is critical for applications
like optimal control, where the control signal is derived from the solution
gradient (Wang & Perdikaris, 2022).

**Solution: Adversarial training** (Wang & Perdikaris, 2022). Replace the
standard $L^2$ PINN loss with an adversarial (Sobolev) loss that explicitly
penalizes gradient errors. This produces better gradient predictions, which is
essential for downstream control tasks.

and its gradient error. Bottom row: adversarial PINN ("Ours") and its

Figure from Wang & Perdikaris (2022).

### Challenge 5: Activation function choice

Since PDEs contain derivatives of the solution, smooth activations that support
accurate first- and second-order automatic differentiation produce better
results than non-smooth ones like ReLU (Dashtbayaz et al., 2024).

## Summary

- **Solving PDEs fast is an important yet open challenge** — the curse of
  dimensionality limits applications in weather prediction, safe robotics,
  material science, and engineering design.
- **PINNs can solve PDEs faster than traditional solvers** if well-designed:
  - Use adaptive sampling rather than mesh generation
  - Learn important basis functions rather than prefixed shape functions
  - Full convergence/generalization proofs are not available yet
- **Key failure modes** are well-characterized:
  - Loss component imbalance (fix: NTK-based adaptive weighting)
  - Convection/reaction dominance (fix: curriculum training, time-stepping)
  - Causality violation (fix: causal weights)
  - Gradient inaccuracy (fix: adversarial/Sobolev training)
  - Activation choice (fix: smooth activations — tanh, sin, cos)
